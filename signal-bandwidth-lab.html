Signal Bandwidth Lab

Pick a waveform and its repetition frequency. The FFT breaks the signal into harmonics at f, 2f, 3f… The bandwidth is how far up you must go to keep most of the signal power. Then put the signal on an optical carrier at 1550 nm (about 193 THz) and see how little of the carrier frequency the signal really takes, and what that means in nanometers.

The signal repeats with period T = 1/f, so its spectrum has lines only at 0, f, 2f, 3f… The line strengths come from the Fourier series (what an FFT of one period gives): square cn = d·sinc(nd), triangle cn = (d/2)·sinc²(nd/2), Gaussian cn ∝ exp(−2π²n²σ²/T²), sine: one line at f. The bandwidth B is the smallest band from 0 that holds the chosen share of the AC power. The red curve rebuilds the signal from only the harmonics inside B, which is what a channel of width B lets through. With the low-pass filter on, each harmonic n is multiplied by the filter response H(nf): an ideal filter keeps the lines below the cutoff and removes the rest; an RC filter has H = 1/(1 + j·nf/fcut), so it also delays and rounds the edges. The red curve is then the filter output. On the optical carrier the signal modulates the amplitude, so the spectrum is copied to both sides of fc = c/1550 nm = 193.4 THz and the optical band is 2B. Δλ = λ²·2B/c, which is the spectral width that enters Δτ = D·L·Δλ.
